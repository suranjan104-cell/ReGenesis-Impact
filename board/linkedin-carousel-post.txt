Australia's mandatory climate reporting threshold falls to A$50m of revenue in July 2027. Singapore moved most of its deadlines back by three years last August. India's assurance requirement reaches the full top 1,000 listed companies by FY2026-27.

Three markets, three different gating rules, one underlying emissions inventory.

I spent the last few weeks compiling what the obligations actually are — the thresholds, the phase-in dates, the assurance ramp, and the supervisory stress tests now sitting behind them. It runs to 26 pages with 10 figures, and every number is referenced to a named source on the endnotes page.

A few things that surprised me while putting it together:

The deferrals are widely read as relief. They aren't. Assurance tests prior-year comparatives, so an entity that starts measuring in FY2030 arrives at its first assured report with exactly the absence of history it would have had in FY2027. The runway moved; the baseline problem didn't.

APRA has now put a number on physical risk that is hard to argue with — expected national weather-peril losses rising from under A$7bn in 2024 to over A$16bn by 2050, and the share of freestanding-property households without home insurance going from one in seven to one in four.

And the NGFS raised projected physical damages two- to four-fold in its November 2024 scenarios. Scenario analysis run on an earlier vintage will not reproduce.

The part I keep coming back to is the mismatch at the bottom of the range. The standards were drafted with large filers in view and extended downward by threshold. The tooling was built for large filers and was not extended downward at all.

If a date or threshold in here is wrong, or has moved since I compiled it, I would genuinely like to know.
